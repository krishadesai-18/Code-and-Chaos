Schools of Thought in Psychology

Course Pack

Psychology as a formal discipline began in 1879 when Wilhelm Wundt established the first experimental psychology laboratory in Leipzig, Germany. Since then, several distinct "schools of thought" have emerged — each offering a unique lens through which human behaviour and mental processes are understood. These schools are not mutually exclusive; modern psychology draws from all of them.

📌 Key Idea
Each school of thought arose as a reaction to the limitations perceived in the previous one. Understanding this progression is essential to grasping how psychology evolved into the integrative science it is today.
Founder: Wilhelm Wundt & Edward Titchener

1. Structuralism

Structuralism was the first formal school of psychology. It aimed to understand the structure of conscious experience by breaking it down into its simplest, basic elements — much like a chemist breaks matter into elements.

Method: Introspection — trained observers reported their inner experiences in a controlled, systematic way (e.g., perceptions, sensations, feelings).

Limitation: Introspection was subjective and difficult to replicate, making it unscientific by modern standards.

Founders: William James & John Dewey

2. Functionalism

Functionalists reacted against the rigid, lab-bound approach of structuralism. Instead of asking "What is consciousness made of?", they asked "What does consciousness DO?" — i.e., how do mental processes help organisms adapt to their environment?

Key Idea: Mental processes serve a functional purpose — they help us survive and adapt. This school was heavily influenced by Charles Darwin's theory of evolution.

Method: Broader observation methods, including studying animals and children, not just lab introspection.

Founders: John B. Watson, B.F. Skinner, Ivan Pavlov

3. Behaviourism

Behaviourism was a revolutionary paradigm shift. Watson argued that psychology should be a purely objective science — and since internal mental states (like thoughts and feelings) cannot be directly observed or measured, they should be excluded from study altogether.

Focus: Only observable, measurable behaviour is the subject of study. The mind is a "black box" that we cannot and should not try to open.

Key Contributions: Classical conditioning (Pavlov), Operant conditioning (Skinner), the Law of Effect, and the concept that all behaviour is learned through interaction with the environment.

Limitation: Ignored the role of cognition, emotions, and innate biological predispositions.

Founders: Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler, Kurt Koffka

4. Gestalt Psychology

Emerging in Germany in the early 20th century, Gestalt psychologists argued that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. They criticised structuralism's approach of breaking experience into tiny pieces, saying that doing so destroys the very phenomenon being studied.

Focus: How people naturally organise and perceive information as unified wholes — patterns, groupings, and meaningful structures emerge automatically.

Key Principles: Proximity, Similarity, Closure, Continuity, Figure-Ground — these are the laws of perceptual organisation.

Insight: They also introduced the concept of "insight learning" — sudden understanding of a problem without trial-and-error (famously demonstrated with Köhler's chimpanzee experiments).

Founder: Sigmund Freud

5. Psychoanalysis (Psychodynamic Psychology)

Freud proposed that most of our behaviour is driven by unconscious processes — desires, fears, and memories that we are completely unaware of. The conscious mind is merely the "tip of the iceberg."

Key Concepts: The Id (primitive desires, operates on the pleasure principle), Ego (rational, mediates between id and reality), and Superego (moral conscience, internalised social norms).

Method: Free association, dream analysis, and analysis of Freudian slips to access the unconscious mind.

Significance: Although many of Freud's specific ideas have been criticised or revised, his emphasis on the power of the unconscious and early childhood experiences on adult behaviour remains influential.

Founders: Abraham Maslow & Carl Rogers

6. Humanistic Psychology

Humanistic psychologists rejected both behaviourism's mechanistic view and psychoanalysis's focus on dysfunction. They argued that psychology should focus on healthy, whole individuals and their potential for growth.

Key Ideas: People are inherently good and motivated toward self-actualisation — realising their fullest potential. Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs placed self-actualisation at the top, above physiological, safety, love/belonging, and esteem needs.

Carl Rogers' contribution was the idea of unconditional positive regard — the belief that every individual deserves respect and acceptance regardless of their actions.

Key Figures: George Miller, Jerome Bruner, Jean Piaget

7. Cognitive Psychology (The Cognitive Revolution)

By the 1950s–60s, behaviourism's strict exclusion of internal mental processes was seen as too limiting. The Cognitive Revolution brought the "mind" back into psychology.

Focus: How people acquire, process, store, and retrieve information. The mind is compared to a computer — it takes in data (input), processes it, and produces output (behaviour).

Key Areas: Memory, attention, perception, thinking, language, decision-making, and problem-solving.

Significance: This is arguably the dominant paradigm in modern psychology and forms a critical bridge to understanding behaviour in organisational settings.

💡 Summary Tip
Remember the progression: Structuralism → Functionalism → Behaviourism → (Gestalt, Psychoanalysis challenged it) → Humanistic → Cognitive Revolution. Each school addressed what the previous one left out.

The Johari Window

Course Pack

The Johari Window is a self-awareness and interpersonal communication model developed in 1955 by psychologists Joseph Luft and Harry Ingham (the name "Johari" is a combination of their first names: Joseph + Hari). It is widely used in organisational behaviour, leadership development, and team-building.

The model uses a simple 2×2 grid based on two dimensions:

  • What is known or unknown to the individual (Self)
  • What is known or unknown to others

The Four Panes

Known to Self
Unknown to Self
Known to Others
🟢 Open / Arena

Information known to both self and others. This is the space of open, honest communication — shared knowledge, behaviours, and feelings.

🔴 Blind Spot

Known to others but NOT to self. These are habits, behaviours, or traits that others see but the individual is unaware of (e.g., speaking too loudly, being defensive).

Unknown to Others
🔵 Hidden / Façade

Known to self but NOT to others. These are personal secrets, private feelings, or information the person consciously chooses not to share.

🟣 Unknown

Unknown to BOTH self and others. This includes subconscious beliefs, latent talents, or potential that neither the person nor anyone else has discovered yet.

Detailed Explanation of Each Pane

🟢 Open / Arena

This is the most desirable and productive quadrant. The larger this area is, the more effective and trust-based the individual's relationships become. Information here includes things like your name, your job role, your openly expressed opinions, and your known skills. When people communicate openly, the Open area expands.

🔴 Blind Spot

This is the area of greatest risk because the individual doesn't know what others perceive. Examples include unconscious biases, communication habits, leadership blind spots, or interpersonal friction caused by behaviours the person doesn't recognise in themselves. Feedback from others is the primary tool for shrinking this area.

🔵 Hidden / Façade

People maintain a Hidden area for various reasons — fear of judgement, lack of trust, or strategic self-presentation. In organisational settings, an employee might hide personal struggles or reservations about a decision. Voluntary self-disclosure is the way to shrink this quadrant — moving information into the Open area requires the individual's own willingness to share.

🟣 Unknown

This is the most mysterious quadrant. It includes things like unconscious beliefs, suppressed memories, untapped talents, and potential future behaviours. This area can shrink through self-exploration, therapy, new experiences, and personal development. As individuals grow, insights from the Unknown may move into other panes.

How the Window Changes

⚠️ Key Mechanism
The Johari Window is dynamic, not static. Two primary actions reshape it:

1. Feedback (from others → reduces Blind Spot): When others share what they observe about you, your Blind Spot shrinks and the Open area grows.

2. Self-Disclosure (from self → reduces Hidden area): When you voluntarily share personal information, your Hidden/Façade area shrinks and the Open area grows.

The goal in healthy teams and relationships is to maximise the Open area through a continuous cycle of feedback and disclosure.

Johari Window in Organisations

In workplace settings, the Johari Window is applied to improve:

  • Leadership effectiveness: Leaders with large Blind Spots may make poor decisions without realising it. 360-degree feedback helps shrink this area.
  • Team trust and collaboration: Teams with large Open areas communicate more honestly and collaborate more effectively.
  • Performance appraisals: Structured feedback sessions help employees understand their Blind Spots.
  • Self-awareness: The model encourages individuals to actively seek feedback and practice vulnerability.
💡 Exam Tip
If asked to explain how to improve the Johari Window, the answer always revolves around two actions: seeking feedback (to reduce blind spots) and practising self-disclosure (to reduce the façade). Both expand the Open/Arena area.

Introduction to Organisational Behaviour

Textbook

Organisational Behaviour (OB) is the systematic study of how individuals, groups, and structures within organisations behave — and how that behaviour impacts organisational performance. It sits at the intersection of psychology, sociology, economics, and management.

Definition & Scope

OB is fundamentally concerned with three levels of analysis:

👤 Individual Level

How a single person behaves at work — their attitudes, perceptions, motivation, job satisfaction, personality, and decision-making.

👥 Group Level

How people behave in teams and groups — teamwork, conflict, communication, group dynamics, leadership, and collective norms.

🏛️ Organisational Level

How the overall structure, culture, climate, and systems of an organisation shape and influence the behaviour of individuals and groups.

🌐 Environment

How external factors — industry trends, competition, legal/regulatory changes, and societal shifts — affect the organisation and its people.

Why Study Organisational Behaviour?

OB is studied because people are the most critical resource in any organisation. Understanding how they think, feel, and behave at work enables managers and leaders to:

  • Improve employee performance by aligning work design with human motivation and cognition.
  • Enhance job satisfaction and engagement, reducing turnover and absenteeism.
  • Manage conflict effectively at individual, group, and organisational levels.
  • Build high-performing teams through understanding of group dynamics.
  • Design better organisations — structures, cultures, and systems that bring out the best in people.
  • Lead more effectively by understanding how leadership behaviour impacts others.

Key Disciplines That Feed into OB

Psychology

Contributes understanding of individual behaviour — perception, motivation, learning, personality, attitudes, and mental health.

Sociology

Contributes understanding of group and organisational behaviour — social norms, culture, conflict, status hierarchies, and power dynamics.

Social Psychology

Bridges the gap between individual and group — studies how people influence one another, conformity, persuasion, and intergroup relations.

Anthropology

Contributes understanding of organisational culture — rituals, values, symbols, and unwritten norms that define "how we do things here."

Economics

Contributes understanding of how people make decisions under resource constraints, incentive systems, and the relationship between compensation and behaviour.

Political Science

Contributes understanding of power, politics, influence, and decision-making within organisational hierarchies.

Core Topics in OB

OB covers a wide range of interconnected topics. The major areas include:

  • Perception and Individual Differences: How people interpret information and how personality traits, values, and attitudes shape behaviour.
  • Motivation: What drives people to work — intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation, theories like Maslow's, Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory, and Self-Determination Theory.
  • Job Satisfaction and Engagement: How organisational conditions influence employee well-being and commitment.
  • Learning and Training: How individuals acquire new skills, behaviours, and knowledge in the workplace.
  • Group Dynamics and Teamwork: Stages of group development (Tuckman's model), roles, norms, and effective team functioning.
  • Leadership: Styles of leadership (transactional, transformational, situational), and how leaders influence organisational outcomes.
  • Communication: Formal and informal communication channels, barriers, and their impact on organisational effectiveness.
  • Conflict Management: Sources of conflict, resolution strategies, and the role of negotiation.
  • Organisational Culture and Climate: The shared beliefs, values, and norms that define an organisation's identity and influence behaviour.
  • Change Management: How organisations adapt to internal and external pressures, and how to manage resistance to change.

OB vs. Human Resource Management (HRM)

📌 Important Distinction
OB is a discipline — an academic field of study focused on understanding behaviour in organisations. It is descriptive and analytical.

HRM is a function — a set of practices and strategies used to manage the human resource, including recruitment, training, compensation, and retention. It is prescriptive and applied.

OB provides the theoretical foundation that HRM uses to design and implement its practices.

Approaches to Studying OB

OB uses both quantitative methods (surveys, experiments, statistical analysis) and qualitative methods (interviews, case studies, observation) to generate knowledge. The goal is always to produce evidence-based management — making decisions based on research rather than intuition or tradition alone.

💡 Remember
OB is not about manipulating people — it is about understanding them so that organisations can create environments where both the organisation and its people can thrive together.

Learning

Textbook

Learning is defined as a relatively permanent change in behaviour or behaviour potential that results from experience (as opposed to maturation, fatigue, or temporary states). It is one of the most fundamental concepts in psychology and organisational behaviour because it explains how people acquire skills, change attitudes, and adapt to new environments.

📌 Key Definition
Learning = a relatively permanent change in behaviour or behaviour potential, resulting from experience or practice. It is NOT caused by: maturation (biological growth), fatigue, temporary motivation, or drugs/alcohol.

Major Theories of Learning

Three broad theoretical perspectives explain how learning occurs:

A. Classical Conditioning (Pavlovian Conditioning)

Ivan Pavlov, a Russian physiologist, discovered classical conditioning while studying digestion in dogs. He noticed that dogs salivated not only at the sight of food, but also at stimuli that were associated with food — like a bell that was rung before meals.

Key Components:

Term Meaning Example (Pavlov)
Unconditioned Stimulus (US) A stimulus that naturally triggers a response without any learning Food
Unconditioned Response (UR) The natural, unlearned response to the US Salivation
Conditioned Stimulus (CS) A previously neutral stimulus that, after repeated pairing with the US, triggers a response on its own The Bell
Conditioned Response (CR) The learned response to the CS — similar (but usually weaker) than the UR Salivation at the bell

The Classical Conditioning Process:

🔔 Bell (CS)
+
🍖 Food (US)
→
💧 Salivation (UR)

After repeated pairing…

🔔 Bell (CS) alone
→
💧 Salivation (CR)

Organisational Application: Classical conditioning explains how employees may develop emotional responses (anxiety, enthusiasm, or dread) toward certain workplace stimuli — such as feeling stressed whenever they hear about a performance review, because past reviews were associated with criticism.

B. Operant Conditioning (Instrumental Conditioning)

B.F. Skinner extended Pavlov's work by focusing on how behaviour is shaped by its consequences. Unlike classical conditioning (which deals with automatic responses), operant conditioning deals with voluntary behaviours. The core principle is: "Behaviour that is reinforced tends to be repeated; behaviour that is punished tends to be suppressed."

The Law of Effect (Edward Thorndike):

Behaviours followed by satisfying consequences are strengthened; behaviours followed by unsatisfying consequences are weakened. Skinner built upon this with his more rigorous experimental framework.

Types of Reinforcement and Punishment:

✅ Positive Reinforcement

Adding a rewarding stimulus after a desired behaviour → behaviour increases.
Example: Giving a bonus for hitting sales targets.

➖ Negative Reinforcement

Removing an unpleasant stimulus after a desired behaviour → behaviour increases.
Example: Removing extra duties once an employee improves attendance.

⚠️ Positive Punishment

Adding an unpleasant stimulus after an undesired behaviour → behaviour decreases.
Example: Issuing a formal warning for misconduct.

🚫 Negative Punishment

Removing a rewarding stimulus after an undesired behaviour → behaviour decreases.
Example: Withholding a bonus due to poor performance.

Schedules of Reinforcement:

Skinner found that how often reinforcement is delivered significantly affects how quickly and how strongly behaviour is learned and how resistant it is to extinction:

Schedule When Reinforcement is Given Effect
Continuous After every desired behaviour Fast learning, but behaviour stops quickly when reinforcement stops (low extinction resistance)
Fixed Ratio After a set number of behaviours (e.g., every 5th sale) High rate of behaviour; brief pause after each reinforcement
Variable Ratio After an unpredictable number of behaviours Highest persistence — most resistant to extinction (e.g., slot machines)
Fixed Interval After a fixed time period (e.g., monthly salary) Behaviour increases just before the reinforcement is due, then drops ("scalloping effect")
Variable Interval After unpredictable time intervals Steady, consistent rate of behaviour (e.g., random inspections)

C. Social Learning Theory (Social Cognitive Theory)

Albert Bandura proposed that much of what people learn comes not from direct experience, but from observing others. This theory bridges behaviourism and cognitive psychology.

Key Concepts:

  • Observational Learning (Modelling): People learn by watching others perform behaviours and noting the consequences. The person being observed is called the model.
  • Vicarious Reinforcement: If you see someone rewarded for a behaviour, you are more likely to imitate it — even though you weren't personally rewarded.
  • Vicarious Punishment: If you see someone punished for a behaviour, you are less likely to repeat it.
  • Self-Efficacy: Your belief in your own ability to succeed in a particular task. High self-efficacy leads to greater effort, persistence, and performance.
  • Self-Regulation: The ability to set personal goals, monitor your own progress, and adjust behaviour accordingly — without external reinforcement.

Organisational Application: New employees learn much of their behaviour by watching how senior colleagues and managers act — especially how they handle difficult situations, treat clients, and respond to challenges. This is why role modelling by leaders is so powerful in shaping organisational culture.

Types of Learning in the Workplace

Type Description Example
Formal Learning Structured, planned learning activities organised by the organisation Classroom training, e-learning modules, workshops
Informal Learning Unstructured learning that happens naturally on the job Learning from a colleague, trial-and-error, reading industry news
On-the-Job Learning Learning by actually performing the work task Shadowing, mentoring, project-based learning
Transfer of Learning Applying knowledge or skills learned in one context to a new context Applying communication skills from a training course to actual client meetings

Extinction and Reinforcement Traps

Extinction occurs when a previously reinforced behaviour is no longer reinforced, causing it to gradually decrease and eventually stop. In organisations, this happens when employees' good work goes unrecognised — over time, they stop putting in extra effort.

Reinforcement Traps occur when managers inadvertently reinforce undesired behaviour. For example, an employee who complains loudly gets immediate attention and resolution — this inadvertently reinforces complaining as a strategy.

💡 Exam Tip — Putting It All Together
When answering questions on learning, always connect the theory to the organisational context:

• Classical conditioning → explains emotional responses to workplace stimuli (e.g., anxiety before audits).
• Operant conditioning → explains how reward and punishment systems shape employee behaviour.
• Social learning → explains how culture and leadership behaviour are transmitted through observation.
